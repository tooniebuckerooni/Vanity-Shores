Entry images, the Act 2 climax, and which romances are real

This document is authored, not generated — the opposite of the game bible, which is read out of the shipping code and must never be hand-edited. Keep them separate: the bible’s whole value is that it cannot drift, and this one’s whole value is that it can be argued with.

The entry-image format

One full-screen painted plate per major character, played once, the first time the player meets them. The machinery already exists — closeup() takes over the screen, holds, and hands control back — so dropping a finished image in is one line of code plus the file.

Canvas
Author at 1280×800, deliver downsampled to 320×200. The game’s logical screen is 320×200 and scales up 3× at run time.
Format
Indexed PNG, 32 colours or fewer, under 40 KB. The palette limit is not nostalgia — it is what makes a painted plate sit in the same world as the procedural art instead of looking pasted on.
Delivery
Base64 data URI, embedded in index.html. The game has no asset folder and should not get one — a single file is what lets it run from a local file, a static host, or an itch upload with no build step.
Duration
Holds until clicked. A name card fades in at the two-second mark.
Safe area
Bottom 40px carries the caption. Keep faces and anything load-bearing above y=150.

Six plates at ~35 KB each adds ~210 KB to a 190 KB file. That is the entire cost of this decision, and it is worth it: these are the only six moments in eleven levels where the game stops and looks at somebody.

Order to commission: Pops first — he is the Level 4 gate and the moment the paid game has to justify itself. Then Duke and Roxy, who carry Act 2. Chip’s flip and the Wildcard come last, because both depend on how the earlier acts actually play.

Marv “Pops” Delgado commission first

The Kingfisher

Dive-bar owner and fixer. Knows everyone. Appears Level 4. Act 2 opens here
The moment
The door of the Kingfisher swings shut behind you and the boardwalk noise cuts out. First frame of the paid game.
Must say
This man was here before the casino and will be here after it. He is not impressed, he is not warm, and he is the only person in Vanity Shores who is not currently selling you something.
Composition
Low angle from just inside the door. He is behind the bar, backlit by the bottles, in the one pool of overhead light. He is drying a glass. He has not looked up.
Palette
Tungsten amber and tobacco brown, hard against the game’s neon purples. The Kingfisher should be the only warm room in the city — that contrast is the character.
Brief for the artist

Interior of a 1980s beachfront dive bar at dusk, painted in the style of a Sierra VGA adventure-game splash screen — 320×200 pixel art, limited palette, hand-painted look, heavy chiaroscuro. Low camera angle from just inside the doorway. Behind the bar: a heavyset Latino man in his late sixties, short-sleeved shirt, forearms like mooring rope, reading glasses pushed up on his forehead, drying a glass with a bar towel. He is lit from behind by a wall of backlit liquor bottles and from above by one caged bulb; his face is half in shadow. He is not looking at camera. Warm tungsten amber and tobacco brown throughout, deliberately opposed to the neon magenta and cyan of the city outside. Details: a dead jukebox, a taped handwritten sign, a framed photo turned face down on the back bar.

Avoid: twinkling eyes, a raised eyebrow, a welcoming gesture, or any other visual shorthand for “wise mentor.” He should look like the bar is closed even though it is open. The warmth is in the lighting, not in his face — that gap is the whole joke, and it is what makes him worth earning.

Chip Winthrop commission last

The passenger seat

Demo antagonist. Flips to ally at Level 4. depends on Act 1 heat
The moment
The flip. He gets into the pink convertible — on the passenger side. You are driving.
Must say
Exactly the same person, now on your side, and somehow that is worse. Nothing about him has improved; the arrangement has.
Composition
From the driver’s seat, over the wheel. He is turned toward camera mid-sentence, one arm along the seat back, entirely at home in a seat he has never sat in before.
Palette
The car’s impossible pink, white upholstery, the sunset doing something obscene behind him.
In-engine
He already has a hand-drawn 44×52 pixel portrait and a close-up treatment. The plate is an upgrade for one beat, not a replacement.
Brief for the artist

Sierra VGA adventure-game splash, 320×200 pixel art, limited palette. First-person view from the driver’s seat of a 1986 convertible in a pink that does not occur in nature, white leather interior, chrome wheel in the bottom of frame. In the passenger seat: a tanned white man in his late twenties, blond, black flat-brim cap worn dead level, black mesh shirt, gold chain, aviators pushed up on the brim. Caught mid-sentence, half-smiling on one side only, one arm stretched along the seat back, utterly relaxed. Behind him a synthwave sunset over a boardwalk strip, magenta into gold. He is lit hot from behind and cool from the dash.

Avoid: any softening. No sheepishness, no truce, no warmth. He has not been redeemed and the image must not suggest it — he has simply worked out that you are a better vehicle than the car.

Note the dependency: if Act 1 left him at high heat, this frame is read as flirtation; if it left him wary, it is read as a hostile takeover. Same image, both ways — which is why the composition must stay ambiguous and the caption does the work.

Duke Calvera commission second

The arm around your shoulders

Rival — “The Scarface.” Volatile. Appears Act 2. carries the Act 2 threat
The moment
First meeting. He is delighted to see you, which is the problem.
Must say
You genuinely cannot tell which way this goes. Both readings — he loves you, he is about to kill you — must be fully available in the same frame.
Composition
Very close, wide lens. He has thrown an arm around your shoulders and pulled you into frame, mid-laugh, head back. The hand you can see is holding a drink. The other hand is out of frame. That absence is the entire image.
Palette
Hot red spill from an unseen source, everything else crushed toward black.
Brief for the artist

Sierra VGA adventure-game splash, 320×200 pixel art, limited palette, high contrast. Extreme close two-shot, wide lens, slightly tilted. A man in his forties in an open silk shirt, gold at the throat, thick dark hair, a scar through one eyebrow, head thrown back mid-laugh with his eyes still on camera. His right arm is around the viewer’s shoulders, pulling them into frame; his right hand holds a lowball glass. His left arm disappears out of the bottom of frame and is not visible. Lit by a hot red source off-screen left, everything else crushed to near-black. Sweat, gold, teeth.

Avoid: a scowl, a weapon, or anything that resolves the ambiguity. The moment a viewer can tell whether he is friendly, the character is dead. He should be the most likeable face in the pack.

Roxy Steele commission second

The mirror

Rival — “The Bait-and-Switch.” Enters as a romance. must lie to the player
The moment
Introduced as a conquest, indistinguishable from the rest of the roster. The image has to work as a straight romance beat.
Must say
Everything a seduction plate should — and it must contain the tell in plain sight, so that on replay it is obvious and on first play it is invisible.
Composition
She is looking at you in a mirror. You see her reflection, warm and open and meeting your eyes. Her actual face, turned away from camera at the edge of frame, is not doing the same thing.
Palette
Vanity bulbs, powder pink and gold, one cold sliver where the real room shows through.
Brief for the artist

Sierra VGA adventure-game splash, 320×200 pixel art, limited palette. A woman in her thirties at a mirrored vanity ringed with warm bulbs, in a hotel penthouse at night. The composition is dominated by her reflection, which is turned toward the viewer, lips parted, eyes soft and directly engaged. At the left edge of frame we see the back and quarter-profile of her actual head — the same woman, not looking at the viewer, expression flat and unreadable. Powder pink and gold from the bulbs; one cold blue sliver of window at the far edge. Everything about the reflection is inviting. Nothing about the real one is.

Avoid: a smirk, a knowing look, a shadow across the eyes. Any telegraphing ruins the turn. The reflection should be the warmest image in the entire pack — it has to be worth falling for, or the betrayal costs nothing.

Madame LaRue already solved

Optional upgrade

Boardwalk psychic. Level 1. Sells the tell that beats the shell game. shipping in-engine

She already has a hand-drawn 44×52 portrait in the build — the storm of black curls, gold at brow, throat and ears, red with a white neckline. She does not need a plate to function.

If you commission one anyway, the moment is the reading: her hands turning yours over, shot from directly above, the table between you. Her face is out of frame or at the very top edge. It is the only image in the pack where the player is the subject — which is the right way to shoot the one character who is looking at the player rather than selling to them.

The Wildcard do not commission yet

Brief for a slot, not a person

Rival — deliberately undefined. Surfaces Act 2–3 from player behaviour. blocked on playtest

The design doc leaves this character open on purpose and names them as the natural first candidate for a goal-driven NPC. Commissioning art now would close the door the design deliberately left open.

  • What decides it: playtest. The Wildcard should be whoever the player has actually been treating as furniture — the character they used and never thought about again.
  • The tell to watch for: which minor character has the most player interactions and the least player regard. The game already tracks enough state to answer this.
  • Composition, whoever it turns out to be: they are looking directly at camera and have been for some time. No action, no threat, no drama. The horror is the eye contact and the fact that the player does not immediately recognise them.
  • Commission this one last, and expect it to be the cheapest and most effective plate in the game.

The Act 2 climax

The design doc puts the Power-act climax at Level 8 as a direct clash with Duke Calvera. You asked about Level 7. I would use both: Level 7 is the chase, Level 8 is the fountain. That split solves two problems at once.

Why a two-parter

  • The doc requires at least one Outrun-style vector set-piece as a deliberate mode switch. It has to be load-bearing or it is a tech demo. Making it the escape that sets up the confrontation earns it.
  • An act climax that is one conversation is thin. An act climax that is one chase is empty. Together they are a chase you barely survive and a reckoning you have to walk into on foot.
  • The pacing break lands where Act 2 sags — the middle of the blend-meter grind.

Level 7 — the chase

Full vector/synthwave mode. Duke does not chase you; Duke is in the car with you, laughing, while the people he has annoyed do the chasing. Your blend-meter position decides who is behind you — the rackets, the law, or the press. The player’s build decides whether you drive, fight out the window, or talk your way through a roadblock. It ends with the car in the water and both of you alive, which Duke finds hilarious.

Level 8 — the fountain

Public. In front of everybody. This is a Level 1 callback: Chip says, in the shipping build, “My family loses more than that in the fountain. There is a man whose entire job is the fountain.” It sits in his first optional beat — the player only hears it by going back and talking to him when the puzzle does not require it, which makes it a reward for exactly the player most likely to still be here in Act 2. Paying it off six levels later — the confrontation with Duke happening in the fountain, with that man standing there watching his life’s work get ruined — makes the whole game feel planned rather than assembled.

optionwhat it costswhat it buys
Chase → fountain Two levels of build; the vector mode needs real obstacle and failure design. Pays off a Level 1 line, delivers the required set-piece, and puts the act climax in public where status actually changes. Recommended.
The yacht One level. Cheapest. A closed room on open water — nobody can leave. Strong for dialogue, weak for spectacle, and it wastes the vector mode.
The count room One level, plus a new interior. You and Duke either side of a table holding more money than either of you. Best theme — but it is a quiet climax at the loudest point of the arc.
The thing to protect: Duke should not be defeated. The doc is explicit that Level 3 ends in an invitation rather than a victory, and Act 2 should rhyme — Duke ends the act deciding you are worth keeping, which is far more dangerous for the player than beating him would be.

Who actually wins

The doc calls for 5–8 pursuable characters and says only 1–2 resolve as a genuine net positive. The interesting question is not who is most attractive — it is what a “win” even means in a game whose thesis is that everything is a transaction.

My answer: the two people who saw you when you were worth nothing. Every other romance in Vanity Shores is priced. Only Chip and Brenda met you on day one, broke, with a philosophy and one bag — and the game already tracks, from Level 1, exactly how you treated them.

charactertieroutcomewhy
Chip Winthrophigh — Act 3 net positive He is the only one who watched the whole arc from the outside. A win here means he chose you after seeing exactly how you did it — which is the one thing money cannot buy in this city. Highest heat ceiling in the game.
Brenda Tanglow — winnable from Act 2 net positive She priced you correctly in your first ninety seconds and came back anyway. She is the only character who never needed you to be anything, and the only one whose route you can close off permanently in Level 1 by intimidating her. That is the game’s best argument for replaying with a different build.
Roxy Steelehigh — Act 3 structurally cannot win She is the bait-and-switch; the betrayal is her function. She should therefore be the most seductive route in the game — the best writing, the warmest art, the fastest heat gain. If she is not genuinely tempting the turn costs nothing.
Duke Calverahigh — Act 3 a win that is a loss Reachable, magnetic, and catastrophic. The ending reads as triumphant and is the worst outcome in the matrix. Good comedy, better warning.
The Trophyhigh — Act 3, gated hardest deliberately empty The one the whole city wants. Hardest to reach, most expensive, and the payoff is nothing — a person who is exactly as advertised and nothing more. This is the thesis stated out loud, so it needs to be earned to land.
Dickie Vermouthnot a romance the control group Plays like one and resolves as friendship. He is the only person in Vanity Shores who wants nothing from you, and the game should quietly reward noticing that.
What this does to Level 1: it makes the heat already shipping in the build load-bearing for the entire game. Chip and Brenda are the two winnable routes, both are seeded in the free demo, and one of them can already be permanently closed by playing Act 1 as muscle. That is the strongest replay hook available and it costs nothing more to build — it is already there.
Sequel hook: the doc requires at least one ending that gestures at Part 2. Use Brenda. Her Level 1 line is already written and already shipping: “When you have real money, come and find me. Not for a unit.” An ending where she finally names what that meant, and it is a business proposition rather than a romance, closes the demo’s oldest thread and opens the next game in one line.
