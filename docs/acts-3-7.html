The back half: seven authoring acts, eleven levels, one hand held out

This document is authored, not generated. It is the second half of the pair that starts with docs/art-and-arc.html — the commissioning briefs, the Level 7 chase into the Level 8 fountain, and the ruling that only Chip and Brenda pay off. Everything settled there is settled here too; this picks up the levels either side of it and carries them to the end. The generated bible stays the ground truth for anything that actually ships.

What this plans and what it refuses to re-plan

Two things are fixed before this document starts. Level 1 ships, and every flag named below is read out of the shipping build by tools/bible.js — if a flag name here does not appear in the bible, it is marked proposed and it is a request on whoever builds that level. Levels 7 and 8 are already designed in art-and-arc, and this document does not touch their construction. It only says what they read on the way in and what they hand forward on the way out.

Authoring acts are not the label on the screen

The handoff sells three acts — Money, Power, Conquest — and that is what the player sees; ACT 1, LEVEL 1 — SMALL CHANGE is already painted on the complete card. The seven acts below are the units this document plans in, and they never appear in the game.

authoring actlevelsthe unit of workplayer-facing

The spine. Level 1's win condition was deliberately changed from reach $100 to make Chip hold what you did with it, and that change is the shape of the whole game. A number can be crossed alone in a room. A person has to be in the room with you. Every level below names its win line as a human being, and the money — the blend meter, the guest list, the count — is always the ticket and never the trophy.
One ambiguity resolved up front. The handoff calls the blend meter a two-pole spectrum (Underworld ↔ Legit) in §5.4 and then names a third “Fame branch” in §5.2 and §6. Treat it as two poles and one amplitude: position decides who engages you, fame decides how loudly. Fame is a multiplier on whichever pole you are standing on, not a third direction — which is why Level 6 can be entirely about fame without moving the needle an inch, and why that is the level's joke.

Level 5 — “Going Concern” authoring act 3

Level 4 hands the player a bar stool, an ally who used to be the antagonist, and a meter with nothing on it. Level 5 is the first time money has to stay somewhere. It is also the first level a low-tier romance can actually resolve, which the handoff gates to Act 2 and the quarantine gates to the far side of the wall.

The first thing that pays you while you sleep

Rackets, laundry, or column inches — pick a door and the other two lean on it. win line: Brenda Tang
Wants
An asset. Not a score — a score is Act 1. Something with a lease on it that is still yours on Monday.
In the way
Duke Calvera prices the boardwalk at four per cent of everything that touches it and considers this generous. The city wants a permit it will not issue to a person with no history. Trish Vandermeer wants a quote, and printing your name is the same as spending it.
Win line
Brenda Tang puts the brochure on the table face up. In Level 1 she wrote something on the back of one, said “when you have real money, come and find me — not for a unit,” and put it in her blazer pocket. The level ends the moment she turns it over and says the second half of that sentence out loud. Nothing else completes it; the money is what buys the meeting.
Ungated
Every build can reach the meeting. Charm gets it in one pass, money gets a better split, fighting gets it by being the only person who ever solved a problem for her without invoicing. No gate is the only route, per the house rule.
If closed
brendaWary — a muscle build that stood over her in Level 1 — locks this permanently, exactly as art-and-arc requires. The slot is refilled, not removed: see the note below.
Reads
brendaWary, gotPremium, signReturned and the brenda secret, heat.brenda, pitchCount, gilPaid, the build split.
Rooms
The Kingfisher back room · the Shoreline sales floor (Brenda's booth, grown up: air conditioning and a scale model of a resort that does not exist yet) · Suds Royale, a coin laundry with a broken change machine · the alley behind the churro cart.

The beats

  • Suds Royale is the launder joke made literal. A coin laundry that takes quarters and gives back quarters, and the entire business is the difference. The change machine has been broken since March and the owner has been feeding it by hand — which means the books are a man's memory, which is why it is for sale.
  • Duke's four per cent is not a threat, it is a compliment. He explains it with an arm around you: “Everything on this boardwalk is mine at four. You are on the boardwalk.” Paying it is fine. Refusing it is fine. Negotiating it is the one thing that moves the meter hard, because it means he has to think about you twice.
  • Brenda's meeting is not a date and must not be staged as one. She has a folder. She has done the arithmetic on you. The romance lands because the proposal is a business proposal — she is the only person in the city who never needed you to be anything, and the way she flirts is by showing you a spreadsheet.
  • The mandatory table: the deposit. Whatever the player buys, they put down more than they are comfortable with in a room where somebody could simply keep it. Level 1's Monte rule, one act up: there is no route to the win line that skips the moment you can lose it.
The replacement route, in full. If Brenda is closed, her slot is filled by Gil Furnace — the sunburnt tourist from the Level 1 bench, back in June, peeling, wearing a Shoreline polo, because after you sold him the aloe he sat through the pitch and bought a unit. He is now the only person in Vanity Shores who owns something and does not know what it is worth. Win line becomes: Gil hands you the keys and thanks you for taking them. Same shape, worse deal, funnier, and it turns the demo's smallest transaction into an act structure. This is the general rule for the back half — a closed route is replaced, never removed.

Level 6 — “The Guest List” authoring act 3

The fame level. It is deliberately the level where the blend meter can move furthest and mean least — you can end it famous and worth exactly what you were on Monday, which is the satirical point and also a fair warning to the player about which axis is real.

Known without being introduced

The Coral Room, the step-and-repeat, and one dead jukebox. win line: Marv “Pops” Delgado
Wants
To walk past a rope without saying a name. Access, which in this city is the only currency that cannot be counted in the count room.
In the way
Duke wants you photographed with him, because a partner in the paper is a partner who cannot leave. Pops wants you out of the paper entirely and will not explain why. Roxy Steele arrives here for the first time and is nothing but helpful, and she is right about everything, and none of it costs you anything yet.
Win line
Pops says your name. Not “kid.” The name the player typed at character creation, out loud, unprompted, in his own bar. It is one say() holding GS.name and it is the most personal line in the game, because the player wrote it themselves and has not heard anybody use it since the bus.
Ungated
The jukebox. It is already in his commissioning brief — the dead jukebox in the corner of the Kingfisher plate. Fixing it is the ungated path and costs no stat: it costs going back to a place the puzzle had finished with, which is the same instinct Level 1's secret rewards. Charm gets the name earlier, fighting gets it after you remove somebody from his bar without being asked, money can simply buy the part.
Reads
heat.chip and chipEnding (Chip is the one who gets you on the list, and how he does it depends on how Level 1 ended), deaths (Trish's first column is about the newcomer who keeps nearly dying), dickiePaid, blend position.
Rooms
The Coral Room, a nightclub with a rope and a man at it · the step-and-repeat outside the Winthrop · the Shore Report's two-desk office · the Kingfisher.

The beats

  • The record on the jukebox is Dickie Vermouth's only single. Of course it is. The B side has been the fourth button on that machine since 1974. When it finally plays, Pops does not comment on it, does not look up, and thirty seconds later uses your name for the first time — and that is the entire payoff, so do not write him a speech. The line is the name.
  • The Act 2 secret is the framed photo turned face down on the back bar — also already in his commissioning brief. Turn it over: Pops at about twenty-five, and a man with a scar through one eyebrow with an arm around his shoulders, head back, mid-laugh, in exactly the composition of Duke's entry plate. The other hand is out of frame there too. Nobody mentions it. It is registered in SECRETS and it is worth more than any payout in the level.
  • Trish Vandermeer will write about you either way. The choice is never whether to be in the paper, it is which of two true sentences she uses. This is the fame amplitude in one mechanic and it costs one dialogue tree.
  • Roxy's introduction must read as a straight romance beat, per her plate brief. She does you a favour here that you did not ask for and cannot repay, and the game should let the player feel good about it for four levels.
Why the win line is Pops and not the rope. Getting into the Coral Room is a puzzle, and puzzles end. The level ends when the one man in Vanity Shores who is not selling you anything decides you are a person with a name. That is the only kind of access the fame axis cannot buy, and putting it at the end of the fame level is the joke landing on the mechanic.

Levels 7–8 — the fixed point art-and-arc owns these

The chase into the fountain is designed. Nothing here re-plans it. What follows is only the interface: what those two levels read on the way in, and what the second half of the game is entitled to assume on the way out.

Level 7 — “Right of Way”

The vector set-piece. Duke is in the car; the people he has annoyed do the chasing. title is a Level 1 callback
The title
The shipping FLATTENED death card ends “Right of way belongs to whoever owns more of the road.” By Level 7 the player owns some of the road. Put the same sentence on the chase's fail card and the joke completes itself six levels later at a cost of zero.
Reads in
Blend position decides who is behind you (rackets, law, or press). Build decides whether you drive, fight out of the window, or talk through the roadblock. monteEnemy puts Monte in the crowd that does not part for you — the one man on that boardwalk with a reason.
Hands out
The car is in the water. Both of you are alive. Duke finds this hilarious and the player's ears are still ringing, which is the correct emotional state to walk into Level 8 with.

Level 8 — “In Front of Everybody”

The fountain. Public. The Act 2 climax. Duke is not defeated
Win line
Duke Calvera decides you are worth keeping, and witnesses hear him do it. Framed as a relationship rather than a victory, per art-and-arc: the terrible outcome is that it works.
The witness
The man whose entire job is the fountain gets a name here: Ruben Ortiz, forty-one years, long-handled coin sieve, municipal windbreaker. He is the only person present who is upset about the water rather than about the money, and his single line is the one the scene is built around. See New faces.
Hands out
Act 3 inherits a player who is owned and popular, a blend position that is now public record, and a Duke who will not be got rid of by any means the game has taught so far.

Level 9 — “Comped” authoring act 5

Act 3 opens and the handoff opens the full roster with it. The level's texture is that nothing costs anything any more: the drinks, the room, the car, the company. Everything is comped, which is the most expensive word in the language.

The room with one window

The penthouse, the floor, and the count room used quietly. win line: Chip Winthrop
Wants
The top of the tower. It has been visible from the boardwalk since the first frame of the game and the Level 1 hotspot already says it: “From here you can see the penthouse. From the penthouse, you are a smudge.”
In the way
You are being comped by four different people who each think that makes you theirs. Angel — the Trophy — is finally reachable and takes an entire level to reach if you let them. Roxy is helping, brilliantly, and asks for nothing.
Win line
Chip takes you up to the room with one window. He described it in Level 1, in an optional beat that is already shipping: a room at the top of the tower with one window facing the whole boardwalk, nobody can see in, “it is a very boring room, you would hate it.” Level 9 is that line coming back. It is boring. One chair, one window, the whole strip below and not one person looking up. He goes there constantly and has never taken anybody.
Ungated
Anyone can be taken up. The ungated route is to ask him for it and spend the favour you were saving — the level hands the player one obviously valuable marker early and the plain path is to burn it on a room with no furniture. Charm, money and fighting each get a version; heat above 60 gets the version where he does not make you ask.
Reads
chipEnding (all five routes; see the payoff table), heat.chip, chipFlirt — whether the player ever heard the tower beat at all — plus blend position and the build.
Rooms
The Winthrop casino floor · the penthouse · the count room · the room with one window.

The beats

  • Both readings of the tower beat have to work, exactly the way Chip's entry plate has to work at high heat and at low. If the player heard the Level 1 line, Level 9 is a promise kept. If they never did, it is a stranger showing them something and being obviously terrified about it. Write the scene so it does not know which one you are having.
  • The count room, finally used correctly. art-and-arc rejected it as the Act 2 climax because it is a quiet room at the loudest point of the arc. Level 9 is the quiet point: you and Chip either side of a table holding more money than either of you, and he counts it one-handed, with his thumb, exactly the way Monte did under the pier — and one of you notices.
  • Angel is a dead end and the level must fund it anyway. The player can spend the whole of Level 9 on the hardest-gated conquest in the game and finish with nothing but an anecdote. That is the thesis stated out loud and it only lands if the pursuit is genuinely good.
  • The mandatory table: the favour. Every route up the tower costs something the player would rather keep, and the game never tells them what it was worth until Level 10.

Level 10 — “Anything You Want” authoring act 6

The turn. Roxy Steele spends what she was given, and the thing to protect is that she never took anything — every single item in her hands was handed over by the player, cheerfully, in a scene they enjoyed.

The city drops you between lunch and four o'clock

Roxy uses the access. Everybody who was comping you stops on the same afternoon. win line: Dickie Vermouth
Wants
Everything at once, which is the state Act 3 has spent two levels teaching the player to want.
What she takes
Not money. Your position. She puts your name on the underworld side of a ledger the press can read and on the legit side of a conversation Duke can hear, and both are true, which is why nothing can be denied. If Level 9 ended in the room with one window, what she uses is the key to that room, which you gave her. If it did not, she takes the Kingfisher's lease instead, and Pops finds out from a letter.
Win line
Dickie Vermouth says yes to something that cannot possibly benefit him. The has-been lounge act from the Level 1 pier — the one the player may have ground for encores at fifteen dollars a time until he refused to sing — is the door that opens. The level ends when he agrees, not when the plan works.
Ungated
Dickie does not check anything. There is no stat gate on the win line of this level at all, on purpose: it is the one beat in the game that is decided entirely by what the player did to somebody who could not stop them.
Reads
dickiePaid, encores — the exact number — readingDone, monteEnemy, brendaWary, heat.*, blend, and whatever Level 9's favour was.
Rooms
The penthouse, emptied · the Shore Report office at deadline · the bandshell out of season · Dickie's room above a dry cleaner.

The beats

  • Madame LaRue's card is the key to the whole level and it is already shipping. The fortune card reads “THE PEA IS NEVER UNDER A SHELL. WATCH THE LEFT HAND, NOT THE SHELLS.” If readingDone is set, quote it verbatim at the moment the player works out that Roxy was never the shell — Duke's hand has been under the table since Level 5. If the player never bought the reading, they get the same realisation two scenes later and nobody says anything clever about it. Twenty-five cents in Level 1, cashed in Level 10.
  • Dickie says the number out loud and the game does not punish it. “Six,” Dickie says. “You had me sing it six times.” Then he gets his coat. That is the entire treatment of the grind path: it is remembered, it is stated, and it changes nothing except how the player feels, which is the only currency this scene deals in.
  • Roxy gets no redemption scene and no villain speech. The best the player is allowed is that she is not surprised to see them still standing. Her plate brief says the reflection has to be worth falling for; the turn has to be worth having fallen.
  • Nobody the player paid for shows up. Angel is unreachable, comps are cancelled, and the only people who answer are the ones who were never on the ledger — which is the argument the ending matrix is about to make.
Climb-out, guaranteed. Losing your position is the back half's version of losing the fifty dollars at Monte's table, and it obeys the same rule: no state the player cannot climb out of. Dickie is the floor. He is always available, he never checks a stat, and he cannot be closed off by anything the player did — the game lets you exploit him, and then makes him the only exit.

Level 11 — “Everything Else” authoring act 7

The protagonist's philosophy, stated on the bus in the prologue, is first you get the money, then you get the power, then you get everything else. Eleven levels later the game finally asks what the third clause was supposed to mean, and the answer is a person or it is nothing.

The settlement, and then the bar after close

Every thread converges; the endings matrix resolves; one coda. win line: whoever will not take it
Wants
To settle. Publicly, expensively, with everybody in one room — the Level 1 curtain run at full scale, because the player learned in the first hour that the way you win in this city is to make somebody hold what you did.
In the way
Everyone left wants a different version of you and they are all on the same floor: Duke, who owns you; Chip, who is invested in you; Brenda or Gil, who is your partner; Trish, who will file either way; and whoever the Wildcard turned out to be, who has been in the background of this level since the boardwalk.
Win line
One person in Vanity Shores refuses your money and stays anyway. The public act is the player trying to pay everybody off, and it works — that is the point, it all works. The ending is decided by who will not take it and is still there in the morning.
The coda
The Kingfisher after close. You put money on the bar. Pops slides it back. “Bar's closed.” Then he uses your name for the second and last time in the game.
Reads
Everything. chipEnding, every heat value, GS.secrets, brendaWary, monteEnemy, deaths, quarterOnGround, GS.name, blend position and the build split.
Rooms
The Winthrop's floor, dressed for an event · the fountain, repaired · the Kingfisher · the arrival stop, one last time.

The beats

  • The quarter is still there. quarterOnGround is live state in the shipping build — a player who never picked it up left twenty-five cents on the tarmac at the bus stop in the first minute of the game. Level 11 goes back to that room. If it is still on the ground, it is still on the ground, and nobody in eleven levels has bent down for it.
  • The last frame is the first gesture, reversed. You hold your hand out, palm up, exactly the way Chip held his out over the quarter and exactly the way the player can end Level 1 — and you wait. It took him eleven seconds. The game counts them again and does not tell you what it is counting.
  • Endings are generated from blend position × romance outcome, per the handoff: mostly neutral, one or two clear wins, none of them a death. Chip and Brenda are the two net positives; Duke is the triumphant-looking disaster; Angel is the empty one; Dickie resolves as friendship and the game should quietly notice that the player noticed.
  • The sequel hook is Brenda's, as ruled in art-and-arc: the ending where she finally names what “not for a unit” meant, and it is a business proposition. If she was closed in Level 1, the hook passes to Gil Furnace, who has read one book about real estate and has ideas, and it is somehow more ominous.

What Level 2 has to record

Level 2 is not built, so nothing below is a fact — these are proposed flag names and a request on whoever builds it. Keep them cheap and keep them about people.

  • l2Crew (proposed) — who the player ran the string of cons with. Read by Level 5, which needs one warm body who will vouch for a stranger buying a laundromat.
  • l2Burned (proposed) — who paid for the stake and did not know it. Read by Level 10, where the ledger Roxy reads from wants a name that is genuinely embarrassing.
  • l2Sabotage (proposed) — how the player answered Chip actively competing. Give it the same five-value grammar as chipEnding so Level 4's flip can read the pair rather than one line.
  • Do not add a second money line. ECONOMY and GATES stay the single source of truth, in cents, and Level 5 onward should add rows rather than figures.

The recurring cast across acts 3–7

Chip Winthrop

Antagonist L1–3, ally from L4, the highest ceiling in the game. net positive available

He does not get better; he gets invested, which is worse and much funnier. The arc is a man discovering that the most interesting thing on the strip is something he cannot buy, and responding by trying to buy it anyway, competently, for eight levels. Level 5–6 he is the door — access is what a Winthrop has instead of a skill. Level 8 is the price of the flip: he is in the crowd at the fountain and he does not help, because a Winthrop does not get into water, and the game must let that sit. Level 9 he takes you up to the room. Level 10 he is what Roxy uses, and he is furious in a way that is entirely about himself. Level 11 he is the best available ending and the only one that requires having been watched the whole way.

Brenda Tang

Timeshare shark, L1. Partner from L5. Low tier, winnable early. net positive · closeable in L1

She is the legit pole of the blend meter with a face on it. Her arc is the smallest and the most complete: she priced the player correctly in ninety seconds, came back anyway, and spends the back half being the only person whose respect is a spreadsheet. Level 5 she proposes. Level 6–8 she is the reason there is anything to lose. Level 10 she does not drop you and does not make a thing of it. Level 11 she names the business and the game ends on a sequel hook rather than a kiss. A muscle build never meets any of this, and that is the single strongest replay lever the demo has.

Duke Calvera

The Scarface. L5 onward, escalating. a win that is a loss

Never a villain, always a problem. Level 5 he prices you at four per cent, delighted. Level 6 he wants you photographed with him. Level 7 he is in the car. Level 8 he decides you are worth keeping, in public, and that is his victory. Levels 9–10 the keeping tightens without a single threat being made — the comps, the introductions, the favours you did not ask for. The Level 6 back-bar photograph is the only thing in the game that suggests he was ever anybody's friend, and it is face down. His romance is reachable, magnetic, and the worst outcome in the matrix; the ending should play as a triumph and be read, on the second playthrough, as a hostage video.

Roxy Steele

The Bait-and-Switch. Enters L6, essential by L9, spends it in L10. structurally cannot win

The discipline is that she never asks for anything. Every item she uses in Level 10 was handed to her in a scene the player enjoyed, and the game must be able to show the receipts. Level 6 she does you an unasked favour. Level 9 she is the reason the hardest doors open. Level 10 she uses the ledger, not a knife. Level 11 she is standing at the back and she is not surprised. No redemption arc, no villain monologue, and — per her plate brief — the warmest writing in the game right up until it stops.

Marv “Pops” Delgado

The Kingfisher. L4 onward. Not a mentor. the ungated conscience

Four states across eight levels, and he never delivers a lesson. L4: he does not look up. L6: the jukebox plays and he says your name. L8: he is not at the fountain, deliberately, and the player should notice the absence rather than be told about it. L10: he may lose the bar, depending on what the player gave away in Level 9, and if he does he says nothing about that either. L11: he slides the money back across the bar. His whole function is to be the one price in Vanity Shores that never gets quoted, and the warmth stays in the lighting rather than the face — the rule his commissioning brief already sets.

The rest of the boardwalk

Dickie, LaRue, Monte, Gil — the demo cast, all of whom come back. cheap payoffs
  • Dickie Vermouth — the control group. Plays like a romance, resolves as friendship, and is the win line of Level 10 because he is the only person the player could exploit without consequence and the game waited nine levels to mention it.
  • Madame LaRue — one scene per act, always already knowing. Her Level 1 card is the thesis of Level 10 and she never explains it. She does not need a plate; her portrait ships.

New faces and what they cost

art-and-arc budgets six painted plates at roughly 35 KB each — about 210 KB on a 190 KB file — and argues that is the whole cost of stopping the game to look at somebody. The back half adds three named characters and exactly one plate. Seven plates is ~245 KB. Everyone else gets a 44×52 in-engine portrait through paintGeneric, which costs nothing and is what the machinery is for.

Palette

Brief for the artist

Sierra VGA adventure-game splash, 320×200 pixel art, limited palette. A man in his late twenties on a white leather banquette in a casino nightclub, shot the way a magazine shoots somebody: centred, symmetrical, evenly lit from the front, nothing hidden. Open shirt, gold at the throat, a tan with no edges, hair that has obviously been paid for. He is looking directly at the viewer with an expression of complete, uncomplicated welcome. Behind him the club is expensively out of focus — champagne, brass, a smear of neon. Warm gold throughout, no shadow anywhere, no rim light, no drama. Everything in the frame is finished. Nothing in the frame is happening.

Avoid: any tell. No bitten nail, no chipped tooth, no flicker of boredom behind the eyes, no sad-behind-the-smile shorthand. Hidden depth is the one thing this character does not have, and the moment the image suggests otherwise the player starts hoping and the whole Act 3 argument collapses. He should be genuinely, expensively lovely and completely empty, and the plate has to be brave enough to be a little boring.

Trish Vandermeer

The Shore Report. Fame amplitude, L6 onward. Low tier, resolves neutral. no plate — in-engine portrait

Four thousand readers, all of them liars, and a column that is the only mechanism in the game for turning behaviour into reputation. She is pursuable, she is never a net positive, and the outcome is the same either way: she writes about you. The choice is only ever which true sentence.

No plate, on the pack's own rule: a plate is for the moments the game stops and looks at somebody, and Trish is never still. She is always mid-sentence and always leaving. Give her a portrait, a blazer with a pen in the pocket, and the fastest dialogue in the game.

The Level 4 quarantine where the crude content actually lives

Levels 1–3 are the free demo. They have to be openable from a link, a static host, an itch page or a hosted artifact with no age gate in front of them, which means the demo's ceiling is innuendo. Level 4 is behind a one-time unlock. That unlock is already a commercial gate, and it is also the only age gate this build needs — so the quarantine line and the paywall line are the same line, drawn once, at Level 4.

What the quarantine changes about pacing

  • The demo cannot spend its best jokes, so Act 1's engine has to be a person rather than the raunch — which is exactly why Level 1's win line is Chip and not a number, and why the heat track is tracked-but-never-resolved. The demo's hook is a relationship it refuses to consummate. That is a feature and it is already shipping.
  • Level 4 carries the whole burden of proof. Everywhere else the game can pace itself; Level 4 has ten minutes to demonstrate that the paid game is a different animal. Front-load it: Pops' plate, the register change, and a cutaway, in that order, before any puzzle.
  • No seduction may resolve below the line. This agrees with the handoff, which gates low-tier conquests to Act 2 and the rest to Act 3, and it means Level 5 is the first level where a romance can actually close. Heat accumulated in the demo is a promissory note the paid game pays out.
  • The camera never moves. Every quarantined beat is still cutaway() — a framed vignette and captions. The quarantine escalates what the caption says, not what the screen shows. That keeps the escalation free (prose costs nothing, a sprite sheet costs weeks), keeps the build shippable on storefronts that would otherwise demand an age gate, and means no new art technology is required anywhere after Level 4.
  • House rules, unchanged in every band: everyone in the cast is an adult, nothing depicts a real person, and the camera stays outside the tent. Reach for on-screen animation only if a joke genuinely cannot survive being described, which so far none has.
The pacing risk to watch. A hard register change at Level 4 can read as two different games stapled together. The fix is that the subject never changes: Act 1 is about what people will hold for you, Act 3 is about what people will hold for you, and the only thing that escalates is how expensive the holding got. If a paid-side scene would still work in the demo with a cleaner caption, it is on-theme. If it only works because it is filthy, it is a sketch.

The properties, at scale re-check these when anything moves

Three rules keep Level 1 winnable for every build. The back half needs a fourth, because Act 1 now writes state that Act 3 reads, and state can close a door that a stat never could.

  • No route to the win line that skips the table. Level 1's version is Monte: no build can reach a hundred dollars without sitting down and risking fifty. Every level below names its table — L5 the deposit, L6 the guest list, L7 the car, L8 the fountain, L9 the favour, L10 the key, L11 the settlement. If a level has no moment where the player can lose what they are carrying, it is a corridor.
  • No state the player cannot climb out of. In the demo the trap is financial and the floor is Dickie's encore. In the back half the trap is social — position, access, reputation — and the floor is still Dickie, who never checks a stat and cannot be closed off. Level 10 is built on this deliberately.
  • No stat gate may be the only route past a progress-critical beat. An even split clears nothing, so every win line above has an ungated path stated in its card. Keep GATES at 38 and differentiate later levels by route rather than threshold; the bible's balance table shows that raising the number narrows all three builds at once.
  • No Act 1 flag may be the only route past a beat either. The new one. Brenda can be closed permanently in Level 1 and Level 5's win line depends on her, so Level 5 ships Gil Furnace as a full replacement rather than a consolation. A closed route is replaced, never removed — otherwise the game's best replay hook becomes its worst soft-lock.
